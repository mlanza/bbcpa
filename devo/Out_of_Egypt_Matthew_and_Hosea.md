# Out of Egypt I Called My Son

*How Matthew connects Jesus to Israel*

Example: Matthew 2:15 connects Jesus coming out of Egypt with Hosea 11:1, showing a pattern between God’s past actions with Israel and what God is now doing through Jesus.

> Matthew: He stayed there until the death of Herod; this happened so that what had been spoken by the Lord through the prophet would be fulfilled: “OUT OF EGYPT I CALLED MY SON.”

> Hosea: When Israel was a youth I loved him, And out of Egypt I called My son.

## The central connection

Matthew 2:15 quotes Hosea 11:1: “Out of Egypt I called My son.” Hosea is recalling Israel’s exodus, not directly predicting a future journey by Jesus. Matthew sees Israel’s history as a pattern brought to its fullest expression in Christ. This way of reading Scripture is called typology.

## Hosea: God’s beloved but unfaithful son

In Exodus 4:22–23, God calls Israel “My son, My firstborn.” Hosea 11:1 remembers God lovingly bringing Israel out of Egypt. But Hosea 11:2 immediately recalls Israel turning to idols. The wider book portrays Israel mainly as an unfaithful covenant people, often using the image of an unfaithful wife (chapters 1–3). The son metaphor is especially prominent in chapter 11. Hosea combines judgment with God’s persistent compassion and promise of restoration.

## Matthew: Jesus relives Israel’s story faithfully

The parallel extends beyond geography. During His temptation, Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6–8, passages tied to Israel’s wilderness testing. Matthew presents Him as the faithful representative of Israel, accomplishing what Israel failed to do.

| Israel | Jesus |
| --- | --- |
| God’s son by covenant and election | God’s beloved Son, understood by Christians as divine by nature |
| Called out of Egypt (Exodus; Hosea 11:1) | Returns from Egypt (Matthew 2:15) |
| Tested in the wilderness for 40 years | Tested in the wilderness for 40 days (Matthew 4) |
| Repeatedly disobeys | Remains faithful |

## What does “fulfilled” mean?

Fulfillment need not mean that the original prophet consciously forecast the later event. Hosea’s words have a clear historical referent: Israel’s exodus. Matthew reads that earlier event as a divinely meaningful pattern completed in Jesus. Christians differ on precisely how prophetic foresight and later recognition relate, but this distinction between original meaning and fuller fulfillment is crucial.

## Why the NASB uses capital letters

The NASB prints “OUT OF EGYPT I CALLED MY SON” in small capitals to mark an Old Testament quotation. The typography does not itself signal Jesus’ deity. Matthew’s identification of Jesus as God’s Son is a theological claim conveyed by the narrative, not by the capitalization.

## Why Jewish and Christian readings differ

Jewish readers can affirm that Hosea 11:1 refers to Israel’s past, without accepting Matthew’s further claim about Jesus. Traditional Judaism also expects the Messiah to bring historical restoration and peace; Christianity teaches that Jesus inaugurated fulfillment and will complete it at His return. The disagreement is not whether the Hebrew Scriptures matter, but whether Jesus is their promised culmination. Jesus, Matthew, Paul, and the earliest Christians were themselves Jews.

## In a sentence

Matthew presents Jesus as the faithful Son who retraces Israel’s story, succeeds where Israel failed, and brings God’s purposes for His people toward completion.

Key passages: Exodus 4:22–23; Hosea 11:1–11; Matthew 2:13–15; Matthew 3:17–4:11; Deuteronomy 6–8; 2 Corinthians 3:14–16.
