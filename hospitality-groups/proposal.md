# Hospitality Groups Proposal

- **Church:** Bible Baptist Church
- **Author:** Mario T. Lanza
- **Date:** October 17, 2026
- **Audience:** Elders

Working draft for elder review and approval.

## Decision Requested

I am requesting approval to launch Hospitality Groups as a standing ministry initiative, with a formal 12-month review for effectiveness, sustainability, and fit.

This is designed as a low-overhead, distributed ministry: volunteer hosts carry most execution, while I maintain system operations and quality control through Planning Center.

## Purpose

We are strong at mobilizing people for ministry, but weaker at creating unhurried space for relationships to deepen.  As a result, people can be highly involved and still feel unknown.  Hospitality Groups are designed to address that gap by creating regular, low-overhead settings where people are valued for who they are, not only for what they do. This initiative aims to strengthen the **relational** fabric — one of our core values — without increasing leadership load.

## Problem / Opportunity

Our church rightly values relational ministry, and many people are genuinely served by current church structures. At the same time, some people remain under-served by those same patterns, even when they are involved and present.

Current touchpoints often create warm but brief interaction. They are meaningful, but they do not always create the repeated, smaller-context rhythm needed for deeper connection. In larger or infrequent settings, people can participate consistently and still remain largely unknown.

The gap is not activity; the gap is intimacy. For some people, most existing pathways feel duty-heavy and cup-emptying rather than relationally renewing. Over time, this can produce relationships that remain thin and situational.

Hospitality Groups address this gap through interest-based, repeatable gatherings where people can know and be known over time. The opportunity is to create a complementary relational lane that strengthens church connection.

## Proposed Solution

Hospitality Groups are interest-based relational groups designed to help people move from casual recognition to meaningful connection over time.

Shared interest is not a side detail in this model; it's the thing that draws people together. People often bond more naturally when they are enjoying the same thing together. In that sense, shared interest helps move relationships from polite interaction toward mutual knowing. As C.S. Lewis observes in *The Four Loves*, friendship often begins when people discover they are turned toward the same thing.

Just as important, the point of gathering is not the activity alone but relationship. Groups must have a relational buffer around the activity so people have real opportunity to know and be known.

The flow is intentionally simple. A potential host brings a group idea to the Hospitality Groups Director. The idea is vetted for fit and appropriateness, then the group is created in Planning Center and the host is onboarded.

From there, church members discover and join groups by interest through normal visibility channels and periodic reminders. Hosts may offer **sessions** and/or **series** depending on the group.

A **session** is a one-time gathering. A **series** is a chain of connected sessions with the same registrants across the season. Series are not too long (think 4-8 sessions), depending on the activity.

Series are intentionally seasonal and intermittent. There are planned lulls and off-season gaps. Some groups may use sessions only; others may use sessions first and then offer a series later.

Sessions and series are driven by RSVPs (using Planning Center registrations), and attendance limits are set by each host based on format, venue, and group goals. The cap gives the host a way to keep the gathering within a reasonable size rather than open-ended attendance. Hosts may keep gatherings intentionally small, choose a larger cap, or use no cap when appropriate. Some gatherings may be scheduled at the church, some in homes, and some elsewhere, depending on the group.

Like a club model, groups may also define a clear demographic fit (for example couples, men, women, families, or shared life stage). This is not exclusion for its own sake; it is a way to improve relational fit and conversational depth.

This model does not recruit by pressure. It adds a low-overhead relational lane where people gather by desire, not obligation, and where intimacy has room to grow.

Illustrative Hospitality Group concepts are listed in Appendix A. Exploratory future service concepts are listed in Appendix B.

## Operating Model

### Lifecycle

This model has a clear lifecycle:

1. host launches group
1. allow time for group discovery and registrations
1. host session(s)
1. host series (stable cohort repeats over time)

### Platform and Structure

Initial implementation uses Planning Center Groups and Planning Center Registrations.

A dedicated `Hospitality` category will be created under Planning Center Groups so this lane is clearly separated and filterable.

### Screening Policy

Hospitality Groups are relational, not doctrinal-teaching groups.  Any proposed group which involves teaching the flock on biblical or moral issues will be referred to the elders for the usual vetting process.

### Pre-Launch Infrastructure

Before launch, the following infrastructure must be in place:

- a Hospitality Groups page where people can discover groups and sign up
- a Hospitality Sessions page where people can view and register for sessions
- clear navigation and visibility from normal church communication channels
- space on the Hospitality Groups page for vision, host onboarding guidance, and my contact information

I will provide the content (vision and onboarding materials). Web support is needed to provide and configure the pages.

### Hospitality Groups Director

I serve as the primary admin and operational owner for this initiative.

I will:

- receive and vet host ideas for fit and appropriateness passing anything subject to the screening policy to the elders
- create Hospitality-category groups in Planning Center
- onboard and train hosts
- shepherd hosts over time
- maintain quality and consistency in the lane

### Host Role

Hosts own local execution within their approved group.

Hosts will:

- define and publish a clear **Group Vision** for their group (see required elements below)
- receive and apply host training that treats shared interest as relational "glue," while intentionally creating space around the activity for meaningful connection
- initiate conversation in their group
- create and run hospitality sessions and series using Registrations
- move from sessions to series only when enough interest is present
- apply the session attendance-limit approach published in their Group Vision

#### Group Vision

Each group’s vision must clearly communicate what people are joining and what to expect.

Required elements:

- **purpose**: what the group is about and what attendees should expect to get out of the sessions; this may distinguish between sessions only and sessions that are part of a series
- **target demographic**: who the group is designed for (for example couples, men, women, families, life stage, or open)
- **session attendance limits**: cap policy for sessions (intentionally small cap, larger cap, or no cap), stated thoughtfully based on relational aims and attendee fit; some people thrive in smaller settings while others connect better in larger ones

### Communications Support Role

Communications support is essential for viability, especially because this model depends on slow, organic interest over time.

Launch communications:

- initiative is introduced on a Sunday with purpose and distinctives
- a short spoken introduction (approximately 5 minutes) can be used

Ongoing communications (once per trimester):

- mention the Hospitality Groups page during announcements
- highlight newly launched groups
- reinforce where people can browse and join
- optionally echo in bulletin and other church communication channels

This is awareness-building, not pressure recruitment: making opportunities visible so people can opt in by genuine interest.

### Reporting and Review

I will provide a list of newly launched groups each trimester for the announcement.

I will also provide an annual report to leadership on participation, movement from sessions to series, and overall fruit/sustainability. At that point, leadership can decide whether to continue as-is, adjust, or stop.

## Leadership Supports Required

To launch and sustain this ministry model, I am requesting the following leadership supports:

1. Approve launch of Hospitality Groups as a standing ministry initiative.
2. Assign web support to build and configure the Hospitality Groups page and Hospitality Sessions page before launch.
3. Assign communications support for launch messaging plus 3 annual reminder mentions (trimester cadence), using the brief I provide and including mention of newly launched groups.
4. Receive one annual report and provide continuation guidance (continue, adjust, or stop).

## Fostering Intimacy

Some Hospitality Groups will begin with sessions and, after people have sampled the offering, move into a series if there is mutual interest and the host believes continuity will serve the group well.

That continuity is the setting where intimacy is most likely to grow: the same people, returning over time, with more room for trust, familiarity, and honest conversation.

But continuity is not required for every group. Some Hospitality Groups may remain session-only by design. That is still a valid outcome. The point is to make room for both paths: one-off sessions where that fits best, and seasonal series where deeper connection is the aim.

## Success Metrics

The success metric is number of **sessions** completed annually.

- **Healthy:** 12 sessions/year
- **Strong:** 24+ sessions/year

If healthy (allow for a half measure in year one) the ministry is working.

Secondary indicators (hosts, registrants, sessions) are useful leading signals, but they are not the main outcome.

Groups may remain quiet for long periods, or close without being treated as failure. Group turnover is expected in an organic, opt-in model.

Ministry-level concern is triggered only if it consistently fails to generate a healthy number of sessions.

Host burnout is not a risk in this design because the model is seasonal, low-pressure, and hosts may pause or close groups at will.

## Appendix B: Example Service Groups

**Lane Definition (Instrumental Value):** Service Groups exist to offer specific acts of help through host-initiated, seasonal opportunities. They are not intimacy-circle substitutes, though relationship can still grow through serving together.

These are exploratory ideas for a *potential future initiative* that applies a low-pressure, seasonal model to service opportunities.

This lane should only be considered after Hospitality Groups has demonstrated success. The current church baseline already contains many service-oriented pathways (serving or being served). Launching service groups too early risks reinforcing the same message: that a person's value is primarily tied to output. The priority of this proposal is to first establish a durable intrinsic-value lane where people are enjoyed, known, and connected apart from performance.

- **Bethesda Mission Meal Service**
- **Visitation**
- **Automobile Maintenance**
- **Yard Work**

Some service groups may use the same lifecycle (host launches group -> registrants -> sessions -> series), while others may remain limited seasonal offerings with a finite number of service slots rather than ongoing series.

This creates room for gift-based service groups. A host may offer a practical skill they enjoy (for example auto maintenance or yard work) as a limited, seasonal offering to church members at no charge.

This is a script flip: the church is not centrally initiating these service opportunities; the host is. Capacity is self-regulated by the host through seasonal limits, small cohorts, and clear start/stop windows. The design avoids open-ended demand and helps prevent burnout without adding heavy admin controls.

Expectation management is essential: registrants should understand that opportunities are seasonal, capacity-limited, and may fill before everyone receives a slot.

This creates a low-pressure way for people to engage service ideas. People can register based on real interest, join a session, and then opt into a short seasonal series if there is fit. It is often easier to sample a service opportunity with a defined end date.

A key added benefit is relational intention among participants themselves. In this model, serving is not only about the people being served; it is also about knowing and being known by the people serving beside you.

This distinction matters: Service Groups center instrumental value (a service output), while Hospitality Groups center intrinsic value (being known, enjoyed, and relationally formed). Both can be good, but they are not the same lane and should not be conflated.

Because this is future-facing, it is treated as optional and experimental rather than part of the initial launch scope.

## Appendix C: Personal Motivation and Rationale

What works for many does not work for everyone.

I believe our church sincerely wants to be **relational**, and I agree with that conviction wholeheartedly. But for some of us, many church social spaces are cup-emptying rather than cup-filling. That does not mean we dislike people. It means we are wired differently. Small talk can feel like work, large-group settings can feel noisy, and connection can remain surface-level even after years of regular involvement.

That has been my experience. I have been present, involved, and committed for a long time, yet meaningful friendship has often remained out of reach. I am not saying this is everyone’s experience. I am saying it is real for some of us, and those people matter too.

I believe one reason is structural. Much of church life is oriented around service, programs, and ministry operations. Serving is good and biblical. Churches should serve. But healthy church life also needs a give-and-take rhythm: breath out and breath in, empty cup and fill cup. When most visible pathways are need-based ("we need you"), and fewer spaces are simply relational ("we enjoy you"), ministry can feel lopsided. In that environment, people can be seen mainly for instrumental value—what they can do—rather than intrinsic value—who they are.

I do not mean service causes burnout by itself. I mean burnout can be a symptom of imbalance: too much emptying without enough refilling through ordinary closeness and friendship that exists apart from task and output.

A greeter can say, “I see you.” That matters. But notice is not the same as depth. A brief touchpoint once in a while cannot substitute for repeated conversation over time. If no one is ever close enough to know you beyond role and function, then relational ministry has not gone far enough.

Jesus said, “By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another.” That kind of witness is strongest when love is not only organized around need, but also embodied in ordinary closeness. Service is one expression of that love. But another expression is just as important: enjoying one another as people, not projects—making room for knowing and being known apart from output.

Hospitality Groups are designed to create that room. Shared interests give people a natural center of gravity. Small, repeat gatherings lower social pressure. Seasonal structure keeps it sustainable. These groups are not one more thing you must do, but an opportunity to connect with others around something you want to do, and probably are doing anyway.

This is why I am proposing this. It is a complement for people who are currently under-served by existing social patterns. My hope is to help create settings where friendships can actually form, where people are valued beyond utility, and where love becomes visible not only in what we do for each other, but in how we choose to be with each other.
